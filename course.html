Start Here Orientation & How to Use This Accelerator

This program is built from a complete 6-week curriculum covering the NDIS ecosystem, business models, marketing, operations, compliance, and your launch plan. It is tailored specifically for you as a registered NDIS provider who is struggling to find clients.

You don’t have to do everything perfectly. The goal is steady progress: each week you put a few building blocks in place so that, over time, you have a professional, compliant and client-attracting NDIS business.

  • Read: Work through the explanations and examples in each week.
  • Decide: Use the checklists to make concrete decisions (services, niche, prices, systems).
  • Capture: Put your thoughts into the notes box for each week – treat this as your live playbook.
  • Test yourself: Use the quick quiz to make sure key concepts are clear.
  • Revisit: You can re-open weeks anytime to refine offers, systems, or compliance as your business grows.

The content was originally designed as a 6-week accelerator. You can keep that pacing:

  • 1–2 focused sessions per week (e.g., 60–90 minutes).
  • Do the reading first, then the checklist and notes.
  • At the end of each week, answer the quiz and mark the week as complete.

If you are in a hurry, you can binge multiple weeks – but you’ll get the best results by implementing as you go, not just reading.

Week 1 Understand the NDIS Ecosystem & Participants
Foundation Goal: think like a participant

In Week 1 you build a solid mental map of the NDIS: who the key players are, how funding flows, and what participants actually want from providers. Everything else in your business – marketing, service design, compliance – sits on this foundation.

The NDIS is a national scheme funding reasonable and necessary supports for people with permanent and significant disability. Two main bodies run the show:

  • NDIA (National Disability Insurance Agency) – decides eligibility, builds participant plans, and pays for approved supports.
  • NDIS Quality & Safeguards Commission – registers and regulates providers, enforces the NDIS Practice Standards and Code of Conduct, oversees complaints, incidents and worker screening.

Key stakeholders you deal with as a provider:

  • NDIS participants – at the centre; they hold the funding and choice.
  • Providers (like HD HeeD) – delivering supports across Core, Capacity Building and Capital.
  • Support coordinators – help participants understand and use their plan, find providers, coordinate supports, troubleshoot issues.
  • Plan managers – manage finances and pay invoices for plan-managed participants.
  • LACs / Early Childhood (ECEI) partners – NDIA partners in the community helping people access the NDIS and connect to services.
  • Families & carers – often heavily involved in decision-making and daily life; treat them as partners (with consent).
  • Mainstream & community services – health, education, transport, community centres, clubs, etc. You’ll often collaborate with them.

Understanding how these pieces fit together makes conversations with coordinators, LACs, plan managers and families much smoother – and helps you position yourself as a trusted, knowledgeable provider.

From the participant’s side, NDIS can feel complex and exhausting. They’ve already told their story to multiple professionals. When they get to you, they mostly want to know: “Will you really listen and will I be safe?”

What participants and families commonly look for:

  • Respect, trust, and safety – feeling heard, valued and not rushed. You explain things clearly, keep boundaries and confidentiality, and genuinely care.
  • Qualified and competent staff – not just “someone with a Cert”, but people who understand their disability, communicate well and know practical strategies.
  • Reliable communication – calls and emails returned, clear updates, no ghosting. This is often the biggest complaint about providers.
  • Personalised, person-centred support – flexibility around goals, preferences, culture and routines rather than a one-size-fits-all roster.
  • Consistency & good fit – minimal staff changes; workers whose style, language and personality feel like a good match.
  • Focus on outcomes and independence – support that builds skills, confidence and genuine progress, not just “filling hours”.
  • Ethical, transparent pricing – clear service agreements, no hidden fees, following NDIS price limits.

Imagine a typical journey with you: how they find you, first contact, intake, day-to-day supports, reviews, and eventually transitions. At each step ask: “How can I make this feel easier, clearer and safer for them?”

Decisions this week

  • Who are your key local stakeholders? (SCs, LACs, community orgs, etc.)
  • What does your ideal participant look like? (age, disability, goals, funding mix)
  • How will you keep your NDIS knowledge up-to-date (sites, newsletters, groups)?

The clearer your mental picture of your ideal participant and ecosystem, the easier marketing and service design become in later weeks.

Week 2 Business Models, Market Demand & Pricing
Strategy Goal: define your place in the market

In Week 2 you zoom out to the NDIS marketplace: how big it is, where demand is strong, and how your registered provider business will position itself – what you offer, to whom, where and at what NDIS-compliant prices.

The NDIS is huge and growing, with hundreds of thousands of participants and billions of dollars of funding. At the same time:

  • There are tens of thousands of providers – many very small – which makes the market competitive in some areas.
  • Registered providers (like you) deliver the majority of overall NDIS spend, especially for NDIA-managed participants and higher-risk support types.
  • Demand is not evenly spread – regional and remote areas, certain disability groups, and some high-intensity or specialised supports often have shortages.

Your goal isn’t to “serve everyone”. It’s to identify where your skills and registration scope intersect with unmet or poorly served demand – that’s where you can stand out.

Your business model answers: “What do we offer, to whom, and how do we deliver it sustainably?”

Key dimensions:

  • Service categories & expertise
    Core (e.g. daily living, community access), Capacity Building (e.g. therapy, support coordination), Capital (e.g. AT, home mods). Choose services where you can maintain high quality with your current skills and registration groups.
  • Target participant group (niche)
    For example: children with autism; adults with psychosocial disability; people from specific CALD communities; participants needing high-intensity in-home support; or aged-care interface participants who need experienced RNs. Specialisation makes your marketing and referrals sharper.
  • Scale and structure
    Solo operator, small core team, or larger multi-disciplinary service? Each choice affects overheads, systems and supervision requirements.
  • Multi-service vs specialist
    “One-stop shop” (e.g. support workers + therapy + coordination) versus being extremely good at one thing. Many successful businesses start focused, then expand services later.
  • Geographic reach
    Local suburb, whole metro area, regional outreach, or state-wide via telehealth / remote services? Think about real travel time, staff availability and local demand.
  • Registered vs unregistered
    You are already registered, which lets you serve NDIA-managed participants and offer higher-risk categories (SIL, behaviour support, plan management, etc., depending on your scope). Use this as a trust signal and market advantage.

Write down your current model honestly: what you actually offer now, who you are really serving, and how. Then adjust it to better align with the niche you want.

The NDIS sets price limits for most supports via the Pricing Arrangements and Support Catalogue. As a registered provider you must:

  • Use the correct support item codes and stay within the published price caps.
  • Understand when you can claim travel, non-face-to-face time, reports, cancellations, etc.
  • Ensure your internal costs (wages, on-costs, admin, supervision, software) still leave a margin at NDIS rates.

Key concepts to build into your system:

  • Support Catalogue – every line item has a code, description, and rate. Set these up in your invoicing / software correctly from day one.
  • Plan categories – Core vs Capacity Building vs Capital. Check each participant’s plan so you bill to the right budget.
  • Fair, transparent pricing – clearly written in your service agreement: hourly rates, any travel charges, cancellation policy, report fees, etc.

Most participants are not price-sensitive in the usual way because NDIS covers the cost. They’re quality-sensitive. Compete more on service quality than on shaving a dollar off the hourly rate.

Bring it together this week:

  1. Review your current registration groups and supports.
  2. List specific services you will actively promote (e.g. in-home support, community access, high-intensity care, etc.).
  3. Define geographic areas you’ll realistically cover.
  4. Allocate NDIS item codes and price caps for each service.
  5. Draft or refine your Service Agreement template to match your real offer and prices.

This becomes the base for your website “Services” page, referral conversations, and onboarding documents.

Week 3 Attracting Clients – Marketing & Networking
Growth Goal: create consistent enquiries

Week 3 turns your understanding and business model into a pipeline of real clients. You’ll sharpen your value proposition, build simple but effective marketing, and map a referral network so that support coordinators, plan managers and community connections actually think of you.

Your value proposition answers: “Why should a participant, family or support coordinator choose you instead of another provider?”

It should be short, specific, and focused on benefits for the participant, such as:

  • Reliability and communication (no one likes being ignored).
  • Specialisation in a niche (e.g. autism, complex health, cultural group, age group).
  • Consistency of staff and a person-centred approach.
  • True understanding of clinical / behaviour risk where needed.

Template you can use:

“We help [type of participant] in [area] to [achieve key outcomes] by providing [main services] with [your special strengths e.g. consistent staff, clinical expertise, cultural understanding].”

1. Brand & messaging

  • Pick a warm, clear tone that matches your value proposition.
  • Use “you” language: focus on what life looks like for the participant.
  • Consistent colours, logo, and wording across website, socials and documents.

2. Website

  • Simple, accessible layout; large text and plain language.
  • Clear sections: About, Services, Areas We Serve, How NDIS Works, Contact.
  • Strong call-to-action: “Call us” / “Submit referral” / “Request a callback”.

3. Social media & content

  • Facebook page is usually the most important for disability community visibility.
  • Post short tips, stories, team intros, and accessible NDIS explanations.
  • Join relevant Facebook groups (participants / carers / providers) and contribute ethically.

4. Community engagement

  • Attend local disability expos, community days, school transition events.
  • Offer free info sessions on topics like “Understanding your NDIS plan”.
  • Drop brochures to GPs, allied health clinics, community centres and schools attached to your niche.

5. Advertising & directories

  • Use targeted Facebook or Google Ads for your area and niche.
  • List your services on NDIS-relevant directories and make sure your Provider Finder info is up-to-date.

For many providers, the biggest shift comes when you stop waiting for random referrals and actively build a network.

  • Support coordinators – map who is active in your area; introduce your service, be highly responsive when they trial you, and send short updates (with consent) on shared participants.
  • Plan managers – register on their provider lists; ensure smooth, accurate invoices so you’re easy to work with.
  • LACs / ECEI partners – ensure they know you exist as an option for particular participant profiles.
  • Other providers – share clients where you have complementary services (e.g. therapy clinic ↔ support work).
  • Community & condition-specific organisations – autism support groups, carer groups, multicultural orgs, etc.

The aim is to be “top of mind” when a coordinator or community worker thinks, “Who is good with this type of participant in this area?”

Marketing to “everyone” makes your message blurry. Marketing to a niche makes you memorable.

  • Use niche-specific language (e.g. sensory needs, executive functioning, behaviour support, complex wounds).
  • Create content that solves that niche’s real problems.
  • Be visible where your niche hangs out (online groups, schools, clinics, cultural centres).

Week 4 Operations & Participant Onboarding
Systems Goal: have a smooth intake & service process

Week 4 makes sure that when participants finally say “yes”, your operations are ready: intake is clear, documents are in order, and your schedule, notes and billing flow properly.

  1. Initial enquiry
    Answer phones/emails quickly, use a simple intake checklist: basic details, NDIS number, plan dates, funding categories, main goals, risks or health issues, referrer details.
  2. Intake meeting
    Confirm needs, goals, and match to your services; explain how your service works. Collect full details and Information & Consent (including consent to talk to SC/PM and to store information).
  3. Welcome pack & service agreement
    Provide a clear pack including Service Agreement, rights & responsibilities, how to give feedback/complaints, your privacy statement, and key contact details.
  4. Support / care plan
    Document their goals, agreed supports, frequencies, risk strategies, communication preferences, and any clinical/behaviour support plans you must follow.
  5. Service delivery & monitoring
    Staff deliver supports; progress notes completed each shift/visit; issues escalated as needed; regular informal check-ins with the participant and referrers.
  6. Reviews & transitions
    Periodically review progress and fit; support NDIS plan reviews; manage planned exits or transitions to other providers safely and respectfully.

Having this lifecycle clearly mapped means every participant gets a consistent HD HeeD experience, not something ad-hoc.

You need systems for at least:

  • Client records (intake forms, consents, support plans, notes).
  • Scheduling (rosters, appointments, reminders).
  • Progress notes and incident records.
  • Invoicing and reconciliation to NDIS line items.
  • Secure storage and privacy.

You can start with a simple stack (secure cloud folders + spreadsheets + calendars) or use dedicated NDIS software. The important part is that everyone knows the process.

  1. Receive enquiry → complete brief intake screen.
  2. Check service fit & capacity → offer intake meeting.
  3. At intake: gather details, confirm goals, explain services and pricing, collect consent.
  4. Complete Service Agreement & create support/care plan.
  5. Set them up in your client record, calendar and billing system.
  6. Confirm start date and roster worker(s).
  7. Begin service; ensure progress notes are completed every shift/visit.
  8. Check in after 2–4 weeks: “How is this going for you? Anything we should change?”

Week 5 Provider Obligations, Quality & Safety
Compliance Goal: feel audit-ready, every day

Registration brings obligations: NDIS Practice Standards, audits, incident management, worker screening, safe environments, financial responsibility. Week 5 is about embedding these into your everyday operations so compliance becomes “how we work”, not a last-minute scramble.

  • Registered provider status means you’ve been assessed against the NDIS Practice Standards and must maintain them.
  • You’ll have regular audits (verification or certification) depending on the risk level of your registration groups.
  • Practice Standards cover areas like governance, risk management, participant rights, provision of supports, incident management, human resources and environment.

Think of the Standards as the blueprint for a high-quality, safe, participant-centred service. Your policies, procedures and day-to-day practice should all line up with them.

  • NDIS Worker Screening for all relevant staff and contractors.
  • NDIS Worker Orientation Module and other mandatory training completed and recorded.
  • Clear position descriptions, contracts, supervision and performance review processes.
  • A training matrix that tracks who has done what, and when refreshers are due.

Participants experience quality mainly through your staff – recruitment, training and supervision are core risk-management tools.

You must have clear systems for identifying and managing risks, handling incidents, and responding to complaints.

  • Staff know what a reportable incident is and how to escalate it quickly.
  • Serious incidents are notified to the NDIS Commission within required timeframes.
  • Patterns of incidents are reviewed, with changes made to reduce risk.
  • Participants know how to complain, can do so safely, and see that you actually act on feedback.

Documenting what you changed after an incident or complaint is powerful audit evidence and builds real safety over time.

  • Track income and expenses clearly by service type and cost centre.
  • Have a simple budget and cashflow plan, linked to realistic utilisation of hours.
  • Ensure invoices match delivered services, line items, dates and rates.
  • Avoid “over-servicing” or billing outside plan scope – it will backfire at review time.

A financially healthy provider is better placed to offer stable, reliable supports – which is exactly what participants and coordinators want.

Week 6 Ready to Launch – Action Plan & Resources
Launch Goal: clear 30–90 day plan

Week 6 pulls everything together into a concrete launch (or relaunch) plan, so you’re not just “registered and waiting” but actively building a stable, sustainable client base.

  1. Finalise your value proposition and services list.
  2. Ensure your website or basic landing page clearly reflects your offer and has a contact pathway.
  3. Contact at least 10 support coordinators / plan managers with a clear intro and value proposition.
  4. Complete key compliance basics (worker screening documented, core policies in place).
  5. Onboard at least one new ideal participant using your refined intake and welcome pack.
  • Continue regular, respectful contact with referrers and update them on participant progress (with consent).
  • Refine systems based on real experience (intake, rostering, notes, invoices).
  • Collect testimonials or case studies (with permission) and add to your marketing.
  • Track key numbers monthly: enquiries, conversions, active participants, hours delivered, cancellations, staff utilisation.
  • Schedule a quarterly mini “quality review” to identify improvements.

Running an NDIS business can feel isolating. Build your own support structures:

  • Join provider networks and forums to share knowledge and stay updated.
  • Follow official NDIS and Commission updates (newsletters, alerts).
  • Consider occasional advice from NDIS-experienced accountants, lawyers or consultants.
  • Invest in ongoing training for yourself and your team.
